Tracy and Roger Beavan live in Burnley. For around ten years they have been Shared Lives carers for Lancashire County Council. Karen was the first person to stay with them for respite after they bought their house. She asked if she could leave her slippers behind. Then she left something else the next time, to be sure she could come back. She has now lived with them for just over two years.

That is the whole scheme in one story, and it is worth explaining properly, because it is not what most people picture when they hear the words adult social care.

## What Shared Lives is

Shared Lives is a care arrangement for adults aged 18 and over with a physical or learning disability. Instead of a care home or a supported flat, the person moves into the family home of an approved carer and becomes part of that household. There are shorter options too: day support, short-term placements, and respite so that someone's usual carer can have a break. Every Shared Lives carer has their own support officer at the county council.

Tracy and Roger started with day support and respite. When Karen's previous Shared Lives carers were moving out of the area she was really upset, so the Beavans asked whether she could live with them for good. Tracy's words: "Karen loves living with us. She cried when we told her she could live here and we've never looked back."

They still offer respite to others, and their home is in such demand that they already have four bookings for 2027.

What I said when the council told their story:

> Shared Lives is such an important scheme as it is creating and expanding families across Lancashire and transforming lives.
>
> Tracy and Roger's dedication to the scheme is clear; from moving house to help more people, to Tracy taking her diary on holiday so that she doesn't miss a respite booking.
>
> We are incredibly grateful to carers like Tracy and Roger, whose compassion and commitment are helping people across Lancashire to live happier and more independent lives.

## Why I keep talking about it

The direction of adult social care in Lancashire is towards people living in ordinary homes, in ordinary streets, with support that fits around them. Shared Lives is the purest form of that. It is a family, not a facility. It is also the form of care that depends entirely on there being enough people like Tracy and Roger willing to open their front door.

If you have a spare room in Burnley or Padiham and think you might be one of those people, take it from Tracy: "This is one of the best jobs I've ever had, and I ever will have." The council's Shared Lives page explains how it works. I would genuinely like to hear from you.
